title: Teaching
---

Whether the course is a general-education class or a 600-level one, a few common threads run through my teaching. I believe understanding how science works, rather than seeing it as a series of facts to be memorized, is a key to scientific and critical thinking. I want students to be able to evaluate scientific claims for their evidence and potential bias, not just to repeat them. My Biology in Popular Media course turns those skills on movies, television, and documentaries. Since the past is the key to the present, evolution and phylogeny are organizing frameworks in several of my courses. Anatomy and physiology make more sense once you see them as the products of historical processes as well as modern ones. I also believe science students should learn to access the scientific literature themselves, so in my upper-level courses we regularly read primary literature, building from a first look at scientific articles in Biology of the Dinosaurs to weekly papers in Comparative Anatomy of the Vertebrates, and student-led, seminar-style discussion in Comparative & Evolutionary Physiology.

In the lab, I teach anatomy like a language: a vocabulary built through daily practice, and then put to work in discussing form and function, with real specimens and dissection at the center. The physiology capstone lab goes a step further, with students designing and running their own experiments and presenting their results as researchers do. Whether you take one of my courses or several, I hope all students emerge with a clearer idea of what science is, and how it works.
