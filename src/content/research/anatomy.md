Before we can ask how an extinct animal moved, breathed, or kept itself warm, it has to be put back together. Much of my work starts there: articulating skeletons, reconstructing soft tissue, estimating mass and its distribution, and testing how alternative reconstructions change the answers downstream.

That work has ranged from sauropod osteology (*Supersaurus*) and the effect of shoulder-girdle placement on sauropod head height, to the mass properties and turning performance of tyrannosaurids (Snively et al. 2019), to the body outline of *Anchiornis* revealed by laser-stimulated fluorescence (Wang et al. 2017).

![Dreadnoughtus skeletal reconstructions comparing mass estimates for articulated and disarticulated rib cages](../../assets/research/dreadnoughtus-mass.jpg "*Dreadnoughtus* mass estimates for articulated and disarticulated rib cages (Hartman 2020).")

Rigorous skeletal reconstructions are also the backbone of my long-running illustration and outreach work, which lives at [skeletaldrawing.com](https://www.skeletaldrawing.com) and is used by museums, textbooks, and other researchers.
